RPT-007: Professional Communications Protocol

Seek first to understand, then to be understood. Speak persuasively, not abrasively. Make it safe.

Overview

This protocol integrates two evidence-based communication frameworks:

  1. Crucial Conversations — For high-stakes, emotionally charged discussions where opinions differ
  2. The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People — For principled, character-based communication

Use Case A: Team Jay (Legal Communications)

Context

Communications with legal team in the United States regarding ongoing matters requiring coordination, updates, and strategic alignment.

The STATE Path — Before Sending

S — Share Your Facts

  • Lead with observable data, not interpretations
  • Use specific dates, numbers, document references
  • Template: "On [DATE], [EVENT OCCURRED]. The documents show..."

T — Tell Your Story

  • Explain your interpretation of the facts
  • Distinguish facts from inferences
  • Template: "My understanding is..." or "I'm interpreting this as..."

A — Ask for Others' Paths

  • Invite their perspective before defending yours
  • Template: "How do you see this?" or "What am I missing?"

T — Talk Tentatively

  • Use conditional language: "I could be wrong, but..."
  • Acknowledge uncertainty: "It appears that..."

E — Encourage Testing

  • Invite pushback: "What's the flaw in this reasoning?"
  • Make it safe to disagree

Safety Protocols for Legal Communications

When emotions run high or stakes increase:

  1. Step Out — Acknowledge when safety is at risk
  2. Make It Safe — Use one of:
    • Apologize: "I'm sorry I wasn't clear about..."
    • Contrast: "I don't mean X, I do mean Y..."
    • Find Mutual Purpose: "We both want to..."
  3. Step Back In — Return to the issue only when safety is restored

Legal Communication Checklist

Before sending any communication to Team Jay:

  • Have I stated the facts clearly, without embellishment?
  • Have I distinguished facts from my interpretations?
  • Have I invited their perspective?
  • Am I speaking tentatively where certainty doesn't exist?
  • Have I made it safe to disagree or provide critical feedback?
  • Is my tone respectful and professional?
  • Have I reviewed for any reactive/emotional language?

Legal Email Structure

Subject: [Matter] — [Specific Action/Update] — [Date]


1. CONTEXT (Facts)

[2-3 sentences on what happened, when, and what documents apply]


2. MY UNDERSTANDING/INTERPRETATION

[How I'm reading this situation — tentative language]


3. REQUEST FOR INPUT

[Specific question or invitation for their perspective]

"How do you see this?"

"What am I missing?"

"Is there another way to interpret this?"


4. PROPOSED PATH FORWARD (if applicable)

[Optional: suggestion with invitation for modification]

"I was thinking we might... but open to your guidance."


5. CLOSING

Professional acknowledgment of their expertise and time.

Use Case B: Physician & External Outreach

Context

Communications to physicians and external stakeholders for professional relationship building, collaboration requests, and information exchange.

The Emotional Bank Account (Covey)

Every interaction either makes a deposit or withdrawal:

DepositsWithdrawals
Keeping commitmentsBreaking promises
Clarifying expectationsCreating ambiguity
Attending to small thingsNeglecting details
Showing personal integrityBeing duplicitous
Apologizing sincerelyBeing defensive or prideful
Seeking first to understandSeeking first to be understood
Being kind/courteousBeing harsh/discourteous

Habit 5: Seek First to Understand, Then to Be Understood

Before Outreach — Research & Preparation

  • What are their priorities?
  • What pressures do they face?
  • What value can you genuinely offer them?
  • What common ground exists?

Diagnose Before You Prescribe

  1. Listen to understand, not to reply
  2. Reflect back what you heard
  3. Confirm understanding before sharing your view

Outreach Communication Structure

Subject: [Specific, relevant subject line]


1. CONNECTION POINT

Brief acknowledgment of their work/expertise/commitment

"I recently read your paper on..."

"Your approach to [topic] resonates with..."


2. MUTUAL PURPOSE FRAME

Establish why this matters to both parties

"We share an interest in..."

"Given your work in X and my focus on Y, there may be..."


3. SPECIFIC REQUEST (if any)

Clear, respectful, time-bounded

"Would you be open to a 15-minute conversation about...?"

"I'm curious about your perspective on..."


4. VALUE EXCHANGE

What do they gain? (Even if just intellectual interest)


5. GRACEFUL EXIT

Easy "out" that preserves relationship regardless of response

"No obligation — I know your time is valuable."

"If this isn't the right time, I completely understand."

Anti-Patterns to Avoid

PatternWhy It FailsAlternative
Long introductory paragraphsPhysicians are busyLead with the ask, then context
Excessive flatteryFeels transactionalGenuine, specific acknowledgment
Hidden agendaBreaks trustBe transparent about purpose
No clear askWastes their timeOne specific, bounded request
Pushy follow-upWithdraws from bank accountPatient, value-first approach

Universal Principles (Both Use Cases)

Start with Heart (Covey)

Before communicating, ask:

  • What do I really want for myself?
  • What do I really want for the other person?
  • What do I really want for the relationship?
  • How would I behave if I really wanted these results?

Learn to Look (Crucial Conversations)

Watch for signs that safety is at risk:

  • Silence: Withholding, masking, avoiding
  • Violence: Controlling, labeling, attacking

When you see these, stop. Make it safe. Then continue.

Master Your Stories

Path to Action model:

See/Hear → Tell Story → Feel → Act

When triggered, retrace your path:

  1. What am I doing? (silence or violence?)
  2. What am I feeling?
  3. What story am I telling?
  4. What are the actual facts?

Then tell a more complete, generous story.

Move to Action

All communications should end with clarity on:

  • Who does what?
  • By when?
  • How will we follow up?

Pre-Send Checklist (Universal)

  • Purpose clear: Do I know what I really want?
  • Safety check: Would they feel safe disagreeing?
  • Facts separated from stories: Have I distinguished observation from interpretation?
  • Invitation present: Have I asked for their view?
  • Tentative tone: Am I speaking as if I could be wrong?
  • Value clear: What's in this for them?
  • Win-win possible: Is this about mutual benefit?
  • Action defined: Is next step clear?

Emergency Protocol: When Conversations Go Wrong

If a communication triggers defensiveness or conflict:

  1. Stop — Don't escalate
  2. Apologize — For impact, not intent
  3. Contrast — Clarify what you don't/do mean
  4. Recommit — To mutual purpose
  5. Invite — Their perspective again
